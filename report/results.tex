\chapter{Results and Discussion}
\label{ch:results}

This chapter reports empirical evaluations across two distinct phases. Section~\ref{sec:clean-eval} first presents the primary clean baseline model selection study, comparing candidate detectors E1, E2, and E3 on the full 44{,}289-clip evaluation manifest across three independent training seeds. Sections~\ref{sec:per-type}--\ref{sec:efficiency} then examine detailed per-generator, shortcut, and regularisation analyses (including FT-RCL M3 and STAR-Edge) conducted during the exploratory evaluation on the 13{,}287-clip evaluation subset using the compact MobileNetV3-Small baseline architecture (E1).

Four key findings emerge from the three-seed baseline selection comparison:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.},leftmargin=*,itemsep=0.3em]
  \item \textbf{Performance superiority of E3:} AudioSet-pretrained PANNs MobileNetV2 (E3) achieves the highest clean detection performance across all evaluated metrics. It surpasses the scratch CNN candidate (E1) by +11.26 percentage points in type-averaged Macro-F1 (0.6274 vs.\ 0.5148), +11.50 points in overall classification accuracy (0.7349 vs.\ 0.6199), and +0.0511 in AUROC (0.8800 vs.\ 0.8289), while lowering the equal error rate from 0.2511 to 0.1993.
  \item \textbf{Stability across seeds:} E3 displays exceptional reproducibility across independent training seeds, with a sample standard deviation of only $0.0032$ in AUROC and $0.0028$ in EER. By comparison, the scratch-trained E1 displays sixteen-fold higher AUROC variance ($\pm 0.0516$) and sixteen-fold higher EER variance ($\pm 0.0460$), reflecting optimization sensitivity to random parameter initialization and stochastic batch sampling under class imbalance. E2 also exhibits high variance in type-averaged Macro-F1 ($\pm 0.0720$) and accuracy ($\pm 0.0846$).
  \item \textbf{Representation differences:} E1 and E2 attempt to learn spectro-temporal discriminative filters strictly from scratch on the 60{,}000 training clips. In contrast, E3 leverages a general-purpose audio representation pretrained on over two million multi-domain clips from AudioSet~\cite{kong2020panns}. This rich acoustic prior enables the network to extract robust, semantically organized representations across environmental sounds, musical instruments, and singing voice, providing a substantially better feature space for the binary deepfake classification head.
  \item \textbf{Parameter and complexity trade-offs:} E3 contains 3{,}537{,}250 parameters, approximately 2.3$\times$ that of E1 (1{,}519{,}618) and 3.0$\times$ that of E2 (1{,}198{,}082). Temporal recurrent pooling in E2 achieves a modest parameter reduction over E1 by collapsing temporal frames into a compact 128-dimensional hidden state, but fails to yield systematic improvements in type-averaged Macro-F1 (0.5039 vs.\ 0.5148). The moderate parameter expansion of E3 is thoroughly justified by its dramatic gains in accuracy and generalization stability.
\end{enumerate}

Based on these verified results, \textbf{E3 is selected as the definitive clean baseline M0} for subsequent research.

This comparison illustrates the scientific positioning of our work:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Internal progression:} Our E1, E2, and E3 candidates establish progressively stronger single-model baselines on AT-ADD Track~2, demonstrating that an AudioSet-pretrained representation (E3) yields substantial gains (+11.26 percentage points in type-averaged Macro-F1 over E1) while maintaining a compact edge-oriented footprint (3.54\,M parameters).
  \item \textbf{Benchmark context:} The published AT-ADD results provide context for the capacity ceiling on this task. \end{itemize}

\section{Generalisation to unseen generators}
\label{sec:generators}

The 38 evaluation generators differ widely in difficulty (Figure~\ref{fig:generators}). At the pooled EER threshold the mean fake recall per generator is 0.78 for the baseline and STAR-Edge and 0.76 for FT-RCL; the number of generators with recall below 0.5 is six, five and four for baseline, FT-RCL and STAR-Edge. Speech generators such as CosyVoice~2, DiffGANTTS and VITS are detected almost perfectly. The hardest are music and sound systems: SSM-TTM is not detected at all by the baseline (recall 0.00), Auffusion reaches 0.12, MeanAudio 0.23 and ACE-Step 0.28. The speech vocoder BigVGAN is also hard (0.43 for the baseline, 0.31 for FT-RCL). A detector trained on 29 generators therefore does not extend to recent text-to-music and sound generators, which fits the weak music and sound results above.

\section{Development versus evaluation scores}
\label{sec:generator-gap}

All three models score 0.91--0.93 Macro-F1 on the official development list, against 0.66--0.67 on evaluation (Figure~\ref{fig:dev-eval}). Overfitting to the development set is not the explanation, as the development set was only used to select an epoch. The cause is the composition of the splits (Section~\ref{sec:overlap}): the development list contains exactly the 29 fake generators of the training list, so its Macro-F1 measures in-generator performance, while the 38 evaluation generators are all unseen. The same gap exists for the full-data baseline (development 0.953, evaluation 0.678). A development set with held-out generators is needed for honest model selection; it can be built from the training list by removing a few generators, and would also make the comparison of checkpoint rules meaningful. We did not rerun the experiments with such a split.

\section{Shortcut cues in AT-ADD}
\label{sec:shortcuts}

We tested whether simple cues alone predict the label. For about 6{,}000 training and 6{,}000 evaluation clips (stratified by type and label) we computed the native sampling rate, the duration, loudness (RMS and peak level), the amount of silence and the bandwidth (95\% spectral roll-off and high-frequency energy ratio). A gradient-boosting classifier was fitted on each cue group on the training clips and applied to the evaluation clips.

All 5{,}786 sampled evaluation clips, real or fake, have a native sampling rate of 16\,kHz, so the sampling rate carries no information and, as far as we inspected, the corpus has no content above 8\,kHz. Loudness, silence and bandwidth cues give an AUROC between 0.49 and 0.60 on the evaluation clips in Table~\ref{tab:shortcuts}, which is close to chance (Table~\ref{tab:shortcuts}). Duration is different: it predicts the label with an AUROC well \emph{below} 0.5 (0.36 over all types, 0.33 for music and 0.32 for sound), which means that the relation between duration and label learnt on the training list is reversed on the evaluation list. In training the fake sound clips are shorter than the real ones (mean 8.3\,s against 9.9\,s), whereas in evaluation the fake music clips are longer than the real ones (mean 11.3\,s against 10.0\,s). A classifier that learnt ``shorter means fake'' on the training list is therefore wrong on the evaluation list. The cues do not show a shortcut that explains the good development scores; they do show that duration differs between real and fake clips in a way that is not stable across splits, which is a risk for models that see more than four seconds.

\section{Degraded-audio pilot}
\label{sec:robustness-pilot}

An exploratory conditioned evaluation was conducted on 100 clips per model, reporting the drop in Macro-F1 under resampling, noise and telephony (Table~\ref{tab:delta-m-summary}). On inspection, this pilot is not methodologically valid and no conclusion about channel robustness can be drawn from it, for the following reasons:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The clips are all fake music.} The evaluation code takes the first rows of the evaluation list, and that list is sorted by type and label. The saved output of the pilot contains only the audio type music, and Macro-F1 of a single-class sample is not defined in a useful way.
  \item \textbf{The zero for resampling is expected.} Every inspected clip is natively 16\,kHz, so the 16\,kHz round trip changes nothing.
  \item \textbf{The negative drops are an artefact.} A negative $\Delta$Macro-F1 of about $-0.3$ under noise means that the degraded audio was scored better than the clean audio. All clips were given the same noise waveform (Section~\ref{sec:degradation}), which shifts the scores of every clip in one direction; at a fixed threshold of 0.5 and with only fake examples, such a shift raises the fraction of clips called fake and hence the Macro-F1. This is a calibration effect and not robustness.
  \item \textbf{No MP3 conditions.} The codec tool was not available, and the code silently returned the clean waveform for those conditions in place of failing.
\end{itemize}

The telephony result, in which FT-RCL loses more than the baseline (0.134 against 0.115), is subject to the same objection and should not be read as evidence for or against the method. A valid test needs a type- and label-stratified sample of the whole evaluation list, per-clip noise, AUROC-based drops, the MP3 condition and conditions that were not used in training (AAC, Opus, G.711 telephony, reverberation, packet loss). \section{STAR-Edge}
\label{sec:star-edge}

The STAR-Edge model was trained for the full 20 epochs and evaluated like the other two models; its results are in Tables~\ref{tab:paired}--\ref{tab:type}. Relative to the baseline it gives the best AUROC (+0.013, interval excludes zero), the best type-averaged Macro-F1 (+0.011) and the best sound and music results, at an unchanged EER and a lower pooled Macro-F1. The improvement on music and sound is the largest effect in this study. Since the signature terms in STAR-Edge were defined on one band only, and it has additional ABR and CAD terms and a different stopping point from FT-RCL, we cannot attribute the gain to any one component. Without the M1 and M2 ablations and without repeated seeds the effect should be treated as a lead to follow up and not as an established result.

\section{Model size and computational complexity}
\label{sec:efficiency}

Table~\ref{tab:efficiency} summarizes parameter counts, FP32 weight storage, and roles across the evaluated models. The scratch-trained CNN candidate (E1) and downstream FT-RCL detector share the compact MobileNetV3-Small backbone of 1{,}519{,}618 parameters (6.08\,MB in FP32 weights), requiring approximately 36.7 million multiply-accumulate (MAC) operations per 4-second clip on the 80$\times$401 input. Temporal candidate E2 replaces spatial pooling and dense projections with a 128-unit GRU, resulting in 1{,}198{,}082 parameters (4.79\,MB in FP32). In contrast, the selected clean baseline M0 (E3, PANNs MobileNetV2) comprises 3{,}537{,}250 parameters (14.15\,MB in FP32 weights).

Three critical points govern the interpretation of these figures:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.},leftmargin=*,itemsep=0.2em]
  \item \textbf{Capacity versus accuracy trade-off:} E3 is larger than the compact E1 and E2 candidates (2.3$\times$ and 3.0$\times$ parameter ratios, respectively). It was selected as M0 not because it is the most compact candidate, but because its clean accuracy ($F1_{\mathrm{type\text{-}avg}}=0.6274$, AUROC 0.8800) and cross-seed stability ($\sigma_{\mathrm{AUROC}}=0.0032$) decisively outperformed the scratch-trained alternatives. As observed by Kong et al.~\cite{kong2020panns} and Combei et al.~\cite{combei2024wavlm}, pretraining on diverse audio corpora provides rich transferable representations; relative to massive transformer SSL models that scale to hundreds of millions of parameters, PANNs MobileNetV2 strikes a pragmatic balance between representation capacity and edge deployability.
  \item \textbf{Exploratory regularisation context:} The downstream regularisation models (FT-RCL M3 and STAR-Edge) were developed upon the compact 1.52\,M-parameter MobileNetV3-Small backbone during exploratory experiments. Applying dual-view consistency losses or multi-objective constraints during training introduces zero parameter or MAC overhead at inference time.
  \item \textbf{Edge suitability requires empirical measurement:} Parameter counts and theoretical MACs alone do not establish edge deployment suitability. Actual on-device efficiency is governed by batch-size-one latency through the STFT and mel frontend, peak runtime memory, memory bandwidth constraints, and integer quantization (INT8) accuracy on target single-board computers or mobile chipsets.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Clean baseline selection and model stability.} E1 demonstrates that a compact scratch-trained CNN can provide meaningful discrimination on unseen audio generators, achieving a clean AUROC of 0.829 across 38 held-out synthesis systems. E2 investigates whether explicit temporal modelling improves this compact representation; adding a recurrent GRU maintains comparable discrimination (AUROC 0.832) with a slightly smaller footprint (1.20\,M parameters) but increases seed-to-seed variance across audio categories. E3 (PANNs MobileNetV2) provides the strongest clean performance ($F1_{\mathrm{type\text{-}avg}} = 0.627$, AUROC 0.880, EER 0.199) and substantially improved seed-to-seed stability ($\sigma_{\mathrm{AUROC}} = 0.0032$ versus 0.0516 for E1), motivating its definitive selection as M0. Consequently, M0 serves as a single, rigorously validated reference model for subsequent experiments.

\paragraph{Why FT-RCL did not help.} The result is negative and we have no experiment that isolates the reason. Several explanations are consistent with the evidence. The signature loss acted on three bands, one row each, so FTSC was a weak constraint; the curriculum never operated, so there was no gradual introduction of hard degradations; the pairs were trained and chosen with a selection score computed on fake music, which makes checkpoint choice close to random among epochs 7--12; and the prediction coherence term has a weak gradient when one view is saturated. Moreover, FT-RCL differs from the baseline only through degradation-related terms while the evaluation measures clean generalisation to unseen generators, which these terms do not target. In that sense the clean evaluation is not a fair test of the idea, and the degraded-audio evaluation that would be one is missing.

\paragraph{What the study supports.} Compact CNN detectors reach an EER of about 16--21\% on speech and singing from unseen generators and are close to chance on music and sound. Development scores from the official split overstate this performance by a wide margin. Under these conditions a small difference between training objectives is within what a single run can resolve for most metrics, with the exception of the AUROC advantage of STAR-Edge and the clean-EER disadvantage of FT-RCL.
